# Script rules (blocky stories)

Never name a game, a game platform or any brand in a title, a line, a sign or on-screen text.

## 0. Whose story is it?
- **The person brought a script:** it is theirs. Voice it word for word. Only split it into lines (one sentence or phrase each). You may list up to 3 suggestions ("line 4 has nothing to film", "the ending comes before the twist"), and change nothing unless they say yes. It does not have to be a town-rule story, start with "In this town" or follow any shape below.
- **They brought an idea or a rule:** keep their idea and their wording of it. Ask one question: "Shall I write it in one of the proven shapes, or do you have your own shape in mind?" Then write it.
- **They brought nothing:** offer ideas across the shapes below (not 10 of the same shape) and let them pick.
- **Any story works in this look:** a school drama, a scary story, a funny skit, a "what if" world, a true story told with block people. The town rule is the default because it is proven, not because it is required.

## The five proven shapes
Measured on five viral shorts (30 to 118 seconds, up to 17 million views). All five open the same way: **the first sentence says the strange thing in under 3 seconds, and shot 1 already shows it happening.** Then the first 10–17 seconds show it on different people, one shot per example, before the main character arrives (15–25 % in).

| Shape | Length | Narrator | Pace | What happens | Ending |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. The rule is the rescue tool** (the default; the method below) | 60–100 s | a kid, "I" | 250–260 words a minute, sentences of about 9 words | The town hides from the rule. Someone is in danger. The narrator uses the rule, small tries fail, the truest version makes the giant one. | A joke that brings back an earlier picture. |
| **B. The greedy one gets caught by the rule's own words** | 90–110 s | the troublemaker, "I" ("My name is ___ and I'm the reason ___") | 250 a minute, about 9 words | The rule has an exact wording said twice early. The narrator cheats with it and wins big. Someone asks one question that turns the wording against them. | A counted punishment: "312 so far. Only 4,000 to go." |
| **C. The town is afraid for the wrong reason** | 100–120 s | a newcomer, "I" | 215 a minute, very short sentences (about 6 words) | 8 funny habits in the first 17 s, one shot each. The newcomer breaks the rule by accident and nothing happens to them. The town panics. The scary thing turns out to be harmless and funny. | Three quick "and now…" jokes that reuse the habits, then a counted last line. |
| **D. The newcomer breaks it (dark twist)** | 35–45 s | a storyteller, "he / she" | 225 a minute, long flowing sentences (about 15 words) | A new kid can't stand the rule and breaks it on purpose. One sentence shows why the rule existed. | A dark joke in one line. Nobody is shown hurt. |
| **E. The cliffhanger** | 25–35 s | a storyteller, "he / she" | slow, 170 a minute, about 7 words | The rule, one person who is different, the rule breaks the world, a stranger says one line. | Cut off mid-event: "…but someone pressed it first." |

Rules for all shapes:
- **Show it, then say why.** Every example of the rule is a thing you can film on one person.
- **The rule's wording matters.** Say it plainly once, and again in other words within 10 seconds.
- **Numbers as digits,** and at least one number that comes back at the end.
- **Speech in quotes** for the two or three lines that turn the story. Other characters may have their own voices if the person wants (ask), else the narrator says them.
- **Third-person stories (D, E):** no character talks to the camera, so nobody lip-syncs except on quoted lines.
- **Safe for kids** in every shape: a monster may appear and people may run, nobody is shown hurt.

The rest of this file is the full method for shape A. For B–E, use its Step 1 (the rule tests 1, 2 and 5), Step 3 (how it sounds, with the pace from the table) and Step 4 (what to give the person).

## The idea in one line

**The rule is a tool.** The town treats the rule as a problem. The narrator is the one person who can use it to save someone, and using it costs them something they were proud of.

## Step 1: the rule

`In this town, every time you [say or do an everyday thing], [one strange thing you can see].`

Rules about something people **say** (a word, a phrase, a kind of sentence) or a feeling they let out (a laugh) work best, because the biggest version can then be the truest thing the narrator has ever said.

A rule is ready when all six are true:

1. **You can see it.** It makes a thing appear: an animal, an object, weather. Feelings and powers don't count.
2. **It's clear in 3 seconds.** One sentence, 10–20 words, the first words of the video.
3. **It has sizes.** A small version makes a small thing, a big version makes a big thing. Say this in the second or third sentence. The ending depends on it.
4. **The size follows honesty.** The biggest version only comes from the truest, hardest thing a person can say or do.
5. **The town hides from it.** People have funny ways to avoid it (tape, helmets, nets, umbrellas).
6. **The giant version is a rescue tool.** Picture the biggest version before you keep the rule: it must physically fix a danger (lift someone, light the way, put out a fire, pay a bill). Also know why the person in danger can't use the rule themselves.

## Step 2: the story shape

Percent of the running time. For 100 seconds, 10 % is 10 seconds.

| Part | Where | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Rule | 0–4 % | The rule, in one sentence. |
| How it works | 4–11 % | What you see when it happens, then the size law: small makes small, big makes big. |
| Funny example | 11–16 % | One person it went wrong for. A number helps. |
| How the town copes | 16–22 % | Two quick pictures of people avoiding the rule. One of them returns in the last line. |
| Me | 22–28 % | "And me?" The narrator is the best in town at avoiding the rule, and proud of it. This pride is what the true line will cost them. |
| The person I love | 28–35 % | A best friend, sister or grandparent, shown through 3 small, funny, specific facts. At least 2 of them come back later, in the tries or the ending. |
| The crisis | 35–42 % | They are in real trouble, with a number that shows normal help is not enough (a ladder too short, money too little, water run out). An adult can say it. |
| The idea | 42–48 % | "Then I got an idea." The rule could save them, but the narrator must do the one thing they never do. |
| Three tries | 48–62 % | Three small attempts. Each makes something a little bigger, each fails, each has a number that climbs. |
| The true one | 62–70 % | "I was out of … Except one." The narrator says the thing they have hidden, about themselves. |
| The giant | 70–80 % | The biggest version of the rule. It works. |
| The catch | 80–88 % | It works too well, in a funny way (too loud, too heavy, it won't stop). |
| The answer | 88–94 % | The loved person answers with the same rule, in one short line, so a second giant appears. The narrator does not comment on it. |
| Last line | 94–100 % | A joke that brings back a picture from "how the town copes". Never a moral. |

## Step 3: how it sounds

- **A kid talking to a friend.** Present tense for the rule, the town, "me" and the loved person; past tense from the crisis on. Short sentences: most are 4–9 words; only the rule may be longer than 18.
- **About 260 words a minute:** words = seconds × 4.3, give or take 5 % (60 s ≈ 260, 90 s ≈ 390, 100 s ≈ 430).
- **Numbers as digits,** at least 8 of them: sizes, steps, feet, dollars, people, days. They go on screen.
- **Speech in quotes** for the tries, the true line, the adult and the answer. Under 20 % of the words.
- **Sound words** on the big moments: "Poof." "Thunder."
- **The narrator's own feelings are shown, not told,** until the true line. That line and the answer are the only places someone says what they really feel.
- **Every line can be filmed:** a person, a thing or an action in each one.
- **Safe for kids:** nobody dies or gets hurt on screen, no swearing, the danger is weather, heights or getting lost.
- **Never:** "little did I know", "that's when I learned", "suddenly", "in a world where", a lesson at the end, or two sentences in a row that start with the same word.

## Step 4: what to give the person

1. **Exactly 6 ideas** as a table: `# | In this town… | What you see | Who is in danger and why the rule can save them | The true line | Last line`. Mark your top 3 and say why: the clearest picture, a giant that really solves the danger, the funniest catch. Each idea makes a different kind of thing (an animal, weather, money, a plant, light, food, furniture, a toy, a door, a vehicle…), and none of them is one of the examples' (balloons, coins, rain).
2. **Stop and let them pick,** or use the rule they gave you. Always offer **7 — Your own story (custom idea)** as the last option. If they pick 7, ask them to provide their rule, idea, or script and **STOP**; never invent one for them. **After the idea is picked, ask for the video length and STOP again:** "How long should the video be? 1) 30 seconds  2) 40 seconds  3) One minute." Do not write the script before they answer. 30 s ≈ 130 words, 40 s ≈ 170 words, 60 s ≈ 260 words.
3. **The script** as plain narration lines in story order, one line per shot: 1–2 short sentences, 6–12 words, about 1.5–2.5 seconds. Put the title and word count above the lines. **Never show the script as a numbered table:** a numbered table is only for the six ideas, and numbering the script lines makes people think they are being asked to pick one. Shot notes (what we see, words on screen) stay in your working draft, not in what the person reads.
4. **The title:** the rule squeezed to 6–9 words, with the visible thing in CAPS: `In This Town, Secrets Turn Into MICE`.
5. **Checks,** listed with a tick or a cross:
   - the rule is the first sentence and makes a visible thing
   - the size law is said before 12 %
   - three facts about the loved person, at least 2 used again later
   - three tries with climbing numbers
   - the true line is about the narrator's own feeling
   - the giant physically solves the danger; the catch is funny; the answer makes a second giant
   - the last line calls back to "how the town copes"
   - the word count fits the length

Then ask in bold, exactly: **APPROVE OR DECLINE SCRIPT?** Then stop and wait for their answer. The person reads and edits the script before any voice or video is made.
